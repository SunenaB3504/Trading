# Investor Guide 1: Foundations and Instruments

Oct 24, 2026 · @Sunilkumar

## 1. Why invest at all

Money left idle loses buying power every year, and investing is the attempt to grow it faster than prices rise. This guide is for learning. It is not advice on what to buy.

**Inflation.** Prices rise over time. At 6% a year, something that costs 100 rupees today costs about 179 rupees in 10 years and 321 rupees in 20. Money that earns less than inflation shrinks in real terms.

**Compounding.** Returns earn returns. The table shows 1,00,000 rupees growing at different yearly rates, before tax and costs.

| Years | At 4% (savings account) | At 7% (bonds, deposits) | At 12% (long-run equity, not guaranteed) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | 1,21,665 | 1,40,255 | 1,76,234 |
| 10 | 1,48,024 | 1,96,715 | 3,10,585 |
| 20 | 2,19,112 | 3,86,968 | 9,64,629 |
| 30 | 3,24,340 | 7,61,226 | 29,95,992 |

The 12% column is an illustration, not a forecast. Equity returns come in bursts, with long flat or negative stretches between them.

**Three different activities.** People use the words loosely, but they differ.

| Activity | Goal | Typical holding | Main risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Saving | Keep money safe and available | Any | Inflation |
| Investing | Grow wealth by owning assets that earn | Years | Falls in value, which may last years |
| Speculating | Profit from price moves | Minutes to weeks | Fast, large losses |

Buying a share because you believe the business will earn more over a decade is investing. Buying it because you think the price will rise by Friday is speculating. Both are legal. Mixing them up is where many beginners get hurt.

**The honest trade-off.** Higher possible return always comes with higher possible loss. An offer of high return with no risk is a warning sign, not a bargain.

## 2. Know your own position first

The right investment depends more on your situation than on the market. Settle these five points before you buy anything.

| Question | Why it matters | A common rule of thumb |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is the money for? | A house deposit in 2 years and retirement in 25 years need different assets | Match the asset to the date you need the cash |
| When will you need it? | Shares can fall 40% and take years to recover | Money needed within 3 years should not sit in equity |
| Do you have an emergency fund? | Forced selling in a crash locks in losses | Keep 6 months of expenses in a savings account or liquid fund |
| Do you carry costly debt? | A credit card at 36% beats any investment return | Repay high-interest debt first |
| Can you afford to lose it? | Risk capacity is about your finances, not your mood | Never invest borrowed money or money for rent and food |

**Risk capacity vs risk tolerance.** Capacity is how much loss your finances can survive. Tolerance is how much loss you can sit through without panic. Use the lower of the two.

**Insurance before investing.** Health and term life cover protect your plan. One hospital bill can force you to sell shares at the worst time.

**A worked example.** A person earns 50,000 rupees a month and spends 35,000. Six months of expenses is 2,10,000 rupees. Until that sits in a safe account, extra money belongs there, not in shares.

**Write it down.** One page with your goal, date, amount and the largest fall you would accept. Reading it during a crash is worth more than any chart.

## 3. Setting up in India

To buy shares you need three linked accounts and a verified identity. All are opened online in a day or two.

| Item | What it does | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| KYC | Verifies your identity once for the whole market | PAN, Aadhaar, bank proof, photo; done through a registered intermediary |
| Bank account | Sends and receives money | Must be in your name and linked |
| Demat account | Holds your shares electronically | Kept with a depository (NSDL or CDSL) through a broker |
| Trading account | Places orders on the exchange | Provided by a broker |

**Brokers.** A broker connects you to the exchange. *Discount* brokers charge low fees and give little advice. *Full-service* brokers charge more and offer research and a relationship manager. Compare total yearly cost, not only the headline brokerage.

**Check registration.** A genuine broker has a SEBI registration number and appears in the exchange's member list. Never open an account through a link sent in a message.

**Settlement (T+1).** A trade done on day T is settled on the next working day. Shares reach your Demat account and money reaches the seller then. Before 2023 it took two days.

**Who holds your money and shares.** Shares sit in the depository in your name, not in the broker's. If a broker fails, your shares are still yours. Cash left with the broker is the part at risk, so do not leave large balances there.

**Power of Attorney (POA).** Many brokers ask for one so they can move shares when you sell. Read what it allows. It should not let anyone take shares or cash for other purposes.

**Nominee.** Add a nominee to every account. Without one, your family faces a long legal process to claim the holdings.

**Contract notes and statements.** After every trade you receive a contract note listing price, quantity and every charge. Keep them for tax filing.

## 4. Equity shares

A share is a part-ownership of a business, and your return comes from two sources: the price rising and dividends paid. Both depend on the company earning money, which no one can promise.

**Size classes.** Under SEBI's scheme, the 100 largest listed companies by market value are *large caps*, ranks 101 to 250 are *mid caps*, and the rest are *small caps*. AMFI republishes the list every six months.

| Class | Typical features | Typical risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Large cap | Established, widely traded, many analysts | Lower, but still falls 30% to 50% in crashes |
| Mid cap | Growing, less covered | Higher swings, longer recoveries |
| Small cap and micro cap | Young or niche, thin trading | Very high; prices can be moved by a few buyers |

**Market capitalisation** is share price times the number of shares. A 100-rupee stock with 1 crore shares is a 100-crore company. Price alone tells you nothing about size or value.

**How you earn.**

- **Capital gain**: you sell for more than you paid.
- **Dividend**: the company shares profit in cash per share.
- **Bonus and splits**: these add shares but not wealth (Section 5 of the Stock Market Data Learning Guide explains why).

**Real cases of how far a share can fall.** These numbers come from the NSE daily data in this project. Each fall is measured from the highest close to the lowest close afterwards, adjusted for splits and bonuses.

| Company | Peak date | Lowest date | Fall from peak |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Yes Bank | 20 Aug 2018 | 23 Aug 2021 | 97.3% |
| DHFL | 3 Sep 2018 | 31 Mar 2020 | 98.7% |
| Jet Airways | 18 Dec 2007 | 22 Aug 2019 | 96.8% |
| Reliance Communications | 9 Jan 2008 | 18 Nov 2019 | 99.9% |
| Suzlon Energy | 8 Jan 2008 | 25 Mar 2020 | 99.6% |
| Aban Offshore | 4 Jan 2008 | 25 Mar 2020 | 99.8% |
| Future Retail | 24 Nov 2017 | 24 Oct 2022 | 99.5% |

Two lessons. Several of these were popular, well-known names when they peaked. And a 97% fall needs a 3,000% rise to get back to the peak.

**Companies also stop trading.** DHFL's last trade in this data is 11 June 2021, Jet Airways' is 23 September 2019, and Future Retail's is 24 October 2022. A delisted share can leave holders with little or nothing.

**Main board vs SME.** SME shares trade in small lots with thin volume. A price move of 20% in a day is normal there, and exiting a large holding can be hard.

**Questions before buying a share.**

1. What does the company sell, and who pays for it?
2. Does it make profit and cash, or only revenue?
3. How much debt does it carry compared with its earnings?
4. Who owns it, and have they pledged their shares for loans?
5. What would make me sell?

## 5. Mutual funds and ETFs

A fund pools money from many investors and a professional manager or an index rule buys a basket of assets for all of them. You own units, not the underlying shares.

| Type | How it works | Traded how | Cost level |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Index fund | Copies an index such as the Nifty 50 | Bought from the fund house at end-of-day value | Lowest |
| ETF | Also copies an index, but trades like a share | Bought and sold on the exchange during the day | Low, plus brokerage |
| Active fund | A manager picks stocks to beat the index | Bought from the fund house | Higher |
| Hybrid fund | Mixes equity and debt | Bought from the fund house | Medium |
| Debt fund | Holds bonds and money-market assets | Bought from the fund house | Low to medium |

**NAV (net asset value).** The price of one unit, calculated once at day end from the value of everything the fund holds.

**Expense ratio.** The yearly fee taken from the fund, shown as a percentage of your money. On a 10% gross return, a 1.5% yearly fee leaves you about 13% less after 10 years than no fee. A 0.2% fee leaves you about 2% less. Always compare the *direct* plan (no distributor commission) with the *regular* plan.

**SIP (systematic investment plan).** A fixed amount goes into a fund every month. It removes the need to guess the right day and builds a habit. It does not remove market risk.

**Tracking error.** How far an index fund or ETF's return differs from its index. Smaller is better.

**Active vs index.** Many active funds lag their index after fees over long periods. That is a general pattern, not a rule, and results change by period and category. Check the fund's own record against its stated benchmark.

**What to read on a fund page.** Benchmark, expense ratio, exit load, top holdings, how concentrated it is, and how it did in 2008 and March 2020.

**Risks.** Equity funds fall with the market. Debt funds can lose value when interest rates rise or when a bond issuer defaults. Past returns shown in advertisements do not predict the future.

## 6. Bonds, government securities and debt funds

A bond is a loan you give to a government or company, which pays you interest and returns your money on a fixed date. The risk is mostly that the borrower fails to pay or that rising interest rates cut the bond's market price.

| Instrument | Issuer | Risk of non-payment | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Treasury bill (T-bill) | Government of India | Very low | Under one year; sold at a discount to face value |
| Government security (G-sec) | Government of India | Very low | Longer terms; pays interest twice a year |
| State development loan | State governments | Low | Slightly higher yield than G-secs |
| Corporate bond | Companies | Varies with rating | Higher yield for higher risk |
| Bank fixed deposit | Banks | Low | Insured up to a limit per depositor per bank; check the current limit |

**Key terms.**

- **Face value**: the amount repaid at maturity.
- **Coupon**: the yearly interest as a percentage of face value.
- **Yield**: the return you actually earn at today's price. It differs from the coupon when the price differs from face value.
- **Credit rating**: an agency's view of the chance the issuer defaults. AAA is strongest; below BBB is risky.
- **Duration**: how sensitive a bond's price is to interest rates. Longer bonds fall more when rates rise.

**The see-saw.** When market interest rates rise, existing bonds with lower coupons fall in price, and the reverse. A bond held to maturity still repays face value, but a bond fund never matures, so its price moves.

**Debt funds.** They hold many bonds. Check the average maturity and the credit rating of what they hold. Funds that chase extra yield by buying low-rated bonds can lose value suddenly if one issuer fails. This has happened in India.

**Worked example.** You buy a 1,00,000 rupee bond paying 7.5% a year. You receive 7,500 rupees a year. If market rates rise to 8.5%, a buyer will pay less than 1,00,000 for your bond, because new bonds pay more. If you hold to maturity, you still receive 1,00,000.

**Where bonds fit.** They usually fall less than shares in a crash and can steady a portfolio. They also lose buying power if inflation exceeds the interest rate.

## 7. Gold, REITs and InvITs

These three let you own something other than company shares or bonds, each with its own way of earning and its own risks.

**Gold.**

| Form | How it works | Points to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gold ETF | Units that track the gold price, bought through a Demat account | Expense ratio; tracks the domestic price |
| Gold fund | A fund that invests in gold ETFs | Extra layer of cost |
| Sovereign Gold Bond | A government-issued bond tied to the gold price, which also paid interest | New issues have been paused at times; check current availability |
| Physical gold | Jewellery, coins, bars | Making charges, purity, storage, and resale discount |

Gold pays no dividend. It earns only if its price rises, and it can stay flat for years. It tends to hold value when shares fall, though not always.

**REITs (real estate investment trusts).** A REIT owns income-producing property, such as offices and malls. By rule it passes most of its rental income to unit holders. You buy units on the exchange, like a share.

**InvITs (infrastructure investment trusts).** The same idea for roads, power lines and pipelines. Income comes from tolls or contracted payments.

| Point | REIT | InvIT |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Owns | Rented property | Infrastructure assets |
| Income | Rent | Tolls, tariffs, contracted payments |
| Traded as | Units on NSE and BSE | Units on NSE and BSE |
| Main risks | Vacancies, rent cuts, rate rises | Traffic or contract shortfalls, debt, rate rises |

**What to look at.** The distribution yield (cash paid as a percentage of price), debt level, how long leases or contracts run, and the quality of tenants or counterparties.

**A caution on yield.** A high payout yield can mean a fall in unit price, not generosity. Compare the yield with the risk-free G-sec yield. If the gap is small, the extra risk may not be worth it.

**Where they fit.** Gold and property trusts often move differently from shares, which can reduce the swings of a whole portfolio. They are still risky and can fall.

## 8. IPOs, rights, preference shares, warrants, commodities and currency

Beyond ordinary shares, several other instruments trade or are offered through the market. Each has its own rules.

| Instrument | What it is | Main risk for a beginner |
| --- | --- | --- |
| IPO | A company's first sale of shares to the public | Paying a high price set by hype; listing gains are not guaranteed |
| FPO / OFS | A further public offer, or an offer for sale by existing owners | Owners may be selling because they see a high price |
| Rights issue | Existing holders may buy new shares at a set price | Deadline; if you ignore it, your stake is diluted |
| Preference share | Pays a fixed dividend and ranks ahead of ordinary shares in a wind-up | Low growth, rarely traded |
| Warrant | The right to buy shares later at a fixed price | Can expire worthless |
| Convertible bond | A bond that can turn into shares | Complicated pricing |
| Commodity derivative | Contracts on gold, crude oil, metals, farm products | Leverage; separate rules |
| Currency derivative | Contracts on dollar-rupee and other pairs | Leverage; small moves mean large profit or loss |

**Applying to an IPO.**

1. Read the prospectus summary (the *RHP*): what the money is for, the company's profits, debts and legal cases.
2. Apply during the 3-day window through your bank's UPI mandate or broker. The money is blocked, not paid, until allotment.
3. If shares are allotted, they appear in your Demat account on the listing date. If not, the blocked money is released.

**Grey market premium (GMP).** An unofficial price quoted before listing. It is not regulated and can be wrong or manipulated. Do not treat it as a prediction.

**Evidence from this project's data.** Among the 644 collapsed stocks in our data that first appeared on NSE after 2007, 62% reached their peak within one year of first trading. A new share can spike on listing and then slide for months. The pattern is a warning about hype, not a rule.

**Anchor investors and lock-ins.** Large institutions are allotted shares before the public and are held to a lock-in. When the lock-in ends, a flood of selling can lower the price.

**Rights issue example.** You hold 100 shares. The company offers 1 new share for every 5 held at 150 rupees, while the market price is 200. You may buy 20 shares for 3,000 rupees. If you do nothing, your ownership shrinks, but you can often sell the right itself during the window.

**Commodities and currency.** These are mainly traded as futures and options. Treat them like other derivatives: Guide 3 explains the mechanics and the risks.
