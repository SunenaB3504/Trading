# Investor Guide 4: Analysis, Risks and Habits

Oct 24, 2026 · @Sunilkumar

## 1. Reading a company: results, ratios and cash

A share price tells you what others pay. The company's accounts tell you what it earns, owes and keeps. Learn to read five numbers before you read any chart.

**The three statements.**

| Statement | Question it answers | Look for |
|----|----|----|
| Profit and loss | Did it earn money this year? | Revenue growth, margins, one-off items |
| Balance sheet | What does it own and owe? | Debt compared with equity and cash |
| Cash flow | Did real cash come in? | Operating cash flow close to or above profit |

**Key ratios, worked on an imaginary company.** Example Ltd has revenue of 1,000 crore rupees, net profit of 100 crore, shareholders' equity of 500 crore, debt of 400 crore and 10 crore shares. Its share price is 150 rupees and it pays a dividend of 2 rupees a share.

| Ratio | Formula | Example Ltd | What it tells you |
|----|----|----|----|
| Earnings per share (EPS) | Net profit ÷ shares | 100 ÷ 10 = 10 rupees | Profit for each share |
| Price to earnings (P/E) | Price ÷ EPS | 150 ÷ 10 = 15 | Years of current profit the price pays for |
| Book value per share | Equity ÷ shares | 500 ÷ 10 = 50 rupees | Accounting value per share |
| Price to book (P/B) | Price ÷ book value | 150 ÷ 50 = 3 | What you pay over accounting value |
| Return on equity (ROE) | Net profit ÷ equity | 100 ÷ 500 = 20% | Profit earned on shareholders' money |
| Net margin | Net profit ÷ revenue | 100 ÷ 1,000 = 10% | Profit kept from each rupee of sales |
| Debt to equity | Debt ÷ equity | 400 ÷ 500 = 0.8 | How much it borrows against owners' money |
| Dividend yield | Dividend ÷ price | 2 ÷ 150 = 1.3% | Cash return at today's price |

**How to use them.**

1.  Compare a ratio with the company's own past and with similar companies. A P/E of 15 is cheap for a bank and expensive for a steel maker in some periods.

2.  A very low P/E can mean the market expects profits to fall.

3.  A high ROE built on heavy debt is riskier than the same ROE with little debt.

4.  Interest cover (profit before interest and tax divided by interest paid) below about 2 is a warning.

**Profit vs cash.** Profit is an accounting number. If profit rises while operating cash flow stays weak for several years, ask why. Customers may not be paying, or income may be booked early.

**Red flags in accounts.**

- Auditor resigns or gives a qualified opinion.

- Debt grows faster than revenue.

- Large unexplained loans to related parties.

- Frequent changes in accounting policy.

- Repeated delays in filing results.

**Where to find the numbers.** Quarterly results and annual reports are filed with the exchanges, and free sites such as the NSE and BSE announcement pages show them.

## 2. Fundamental and technical analysis

Fundamental analysis asks what a business is worth. Technical analysis studies price and volume patterns. Neither predicts the future reliably, and each has uses and limits.

| Aspect | Fundamental analysis | Technical analysis |
|----|----|----|
| Studies | Accounts, industry, management, competition | Price charts, volume, indicators |
| Typical time frame | Years | Days to months |
| Main idea | Price eventually follows value | Past patterns repeat |
| Strength | Explains why a company may do well or badly | Gives clear entry and exit rules |
| Weakness | Value is an estimate; markets can ignore it for years | Patterns are easy to see in hindsight and often fail |
| Common tools | Ratios, cash-flow models, peer comparison | Trend lines, moving averages, RSI, support and resistance |

**Terms from technical analysis.**

- **Moving average**: the average price over the last N days. A price above its 200-day average is called an uptrend.

- **Support / resistance**: price levels where buyers or sellers have appeared before.

- **RSI (relative strength index)**: a 0 to 100 gauge of recent gains against recent losses. Readings above 70 are called overbought and below 30 oversold.

- **Volume**: confirms whether a move has many participants.

**What the evidence says.** Careful research tests a rule on data it was not designed on and counts how many rules were tried. Many published patterns lose their edge once costs are included. A pattern that worked in one period may fail in the next. Be especially careful with a pattern found by looking at a chart, since you can see a pattern in almost any chart.

**Useful mix.**

1.  Use fundamentals to decide *whether* to own a business.

2.  Use price and volume to avoid illiquid stocks and to avoid buying into a collapse.

3.  Write down the rule for entry and exit before you trade, and test it on old data you did not use to design it.

**A real warning from our data.** Aban Offshore, Suzlon Energy and Reliance Communications all fell more than 99% from their January 2008 highs, and each looked cheap against its old price along the way down. A lower price is not by itself a sign of value. Look at debt and cash flow before treating a fallen stock as an opportunity.

**Other approaches.** Quantitative investing uses rules over many stocks, such as buying those with high profit growth. Index investing skips the choice and buys the whole market. Both are better tested than most chart patterns.

## 3. Who owns the company: promoters, pledges and governance

In India, one family or group, called the promoter, often owns a large share of a listed company. How they behave matters as much as the business, because minority shareholders depend on their honesty.

**Shareholding pattern.** Every listed company files a quarterly statement showing who owns what.

| Holder | What it tells you |
|----|----|
| Promoters | Their stake and whether it is rising or falling |
| Foreign institutional investors (FII / FPI) | Whether global money is buying or selling |
| Domestic institutions (mutual funds, insurers) | Professional interest |
| Public | Retail and other holders |

**Promoter pledging.** A promoter may pledge shares to a lender as security for a loan. If the share price falls, the lender can sell the pledged shares, which pushes the price down further. High pledging, such as more than 30% to 50% of the promoter's holding, is a warning.

**Worked example: the pledge spiral.** A promoter holds 50% of a company and pledges 80% of that stake to borrow money. The price falls 30%, so the lender asks for more security. The promoter cannot pay, and the lender sells shares in the market. The price falls further and triggers more selling. Several Indian companies have followed this path.

**Related-party dealings.** Money lent to or business done with companies the promoter controls can move value out of the listed company. Look in the annual report for notes titled 'related party transactions'.

**Governance checklist.**

1.  Is the auditor well known and stable?

2.  Are independent directors truly independent?

3.  Has the company delayed results or changed the auditor suddenly?

4.  Are there regulatory orders or court cases against the company or promoters?

5.  Does management communicate openly in calls and filings?

**What happened to well-known names.** From NSE data in this project, the last trading day of DHFL is 11 June 2021, of Jet Airways 23 September 2019, and of Future Retail 24 October 2022. By their lowest closes, each had fallen more than 95% from its peak. I make no claim here about causes. Read each company's filings and the news from the time to see how the warning signs appeared.

**Habit.** Before buying a small or mid-sized company, read the last four shareholding patterns and the notes on related-party transactions.

## 4. Types of risk, with real evidence

Risk means the chance of losing money or not reaching your goal. It comes in several kinds, and the biggest one for a stock buyer is the one beginners think least about: the company itself failing.

| Risk | Meaning | Example |
|----|----|----|
| Market risk | The whole market falls | Nifty fell 59.9% from January to October 2008 |
| Company risk | One business does badly | Yes Bank fell 97.3% from August 2018 to August 2021 |
| Concentration risk | Too much money in one stock or sector | Holding only one of the names that collapsed |
| Liquidity risk | You cannot sell without moving the price | Aban Offshore traded only 9,969 shares on 16 October 2026 |
| Leverage risk | Borrowed money magnifies a loss | MTF and F&O (Guides 2 and 3) |
| Inflation risk | Returns do not keep up with prices | Savings account at 4% against 6% inflation |
| Interest-rate risk | Bond prices fall when rates rise | Guide 1, section 6 |
| Currency risk | The rupee moves against a foreign asset | Foreign funds and shares |
| Regulatory risk | A rule change hurts a business or product | Tax changes, SEBI orders |
| Operational risk | Mistakes, outages, fraud | Wrong order, broker failure |

**How common is a collapse?** In this project we examined every NSE stock with at least 250 trading days between 2007 and October 2026, which is 3,616 stocks. Of them:

- **1,022 stocks (28%)** fell at least 70% from a peak, never regained half of that peak, and ended the data at 40% or less of it.

- **889 of those** had no unexplained one-day drop worse than 35%, which makes a corporate event such as a merger less likely to be the cause.

These are not 889 frauds. Many were small, thin companies, and some were ordinary failures of a business. They show that a long-lived stock falling 70% and staying down is a common outcome in this market, not a freak event.

**Survivorship and this evidence.** The Nifty 50 index continually replaces failing companies with stronger ones, so its history looks better than the history of a fixed set of stocks. An investor who held the same 50 names from 2007 would have had a different result.

**Diversification.** Holding many unrelated stocks reduces company risk. If one in four stocks can fail badly, a portfolio of three stocks has a good chance of including one failure. A broad index fund holds hundreds of companies and updates the list.

**Worked example: a small portfolio.** You put 1,00,000 rupees equally in 5 stocks, 20,000 each. One falls 90%, and the other four rise 10% each. Result: 18,000 + 4 times 22,000 = 1,06,000. A single stock that falls 90% would have cost you 90,000.

**Position sizing.** If you cap each stock at 5% of your savings, a 90% loss costs 4.5% of the total. That is survivable. At 50% it costs 45%.

## 5. Market structure risks: circuits, halts, surveillance

The market itself has rules that can stop you from selling when you most want to. Learn them before you hold a stock you cannot afford to be stuck in.

| Mechanism | What it does | Risk to you |
|----|----|----|
| Stock circuit limit | Stops trading in one direction once the price moves a set percentage | In a falling stock you may be unable to sell at all that day |
| Lower band on surveillance names | The limit is cut to 5% or even 2% | A long wait to exit if the stock keeps falling |
| Trade-for-trade (BE series) | No intraday trading; all trades go to delivery | Less liquidity |
| ASM / GSM lists | Extra margin and surveillance when prices look abnormal | A warning of unusual activity |
| Suspension | Trading stopped for violations or missing filings | No exit until reinstated |
| Delisting | A company leaves the exchange | Forced sale at a set price, or no market |
| Index circuit breakers | All trading halts after a 10%, 15% or 20% index fall | A halt during a crash |

**Real cases from NSE data.**

- Aban Offshore now trades in the BE series. On 16 October 2026 only 9,969 shares changed hands.

- DHFL, Jet Airways and Future Retail stopped trading on 11 June 2021, 23 September 2019 and 24 October 2022, with their prices down more than 95% from their peaks.

- On 23 March 2020 the Nifty fell 13.0% in one day, the largest fall in our data.

**Operator-driven stocks.** Some small stocks are pushed up by a few traders and then dumped on late buyers. Signs: price rising on tiny volume, no news, constant upper circuits, heavy WhatsApp or Telegram promotion.

**Worked example: stuck in a lower circuit.** A stock is at 100 and its band is 5%. It falls to 95 and hits the lower circuit with a million sell orders and no buyers. You cannot sell at 95. Next day it opens at 95 and falls to 90.25, again at the limit. You could be stuck for many days while the price slides.

**What to do.**

1.  Check a stock's band and average volume before buying.

2.  Avoid stocks on surveillance lists unless you understand why.

3.  Keep illiquid holdings small.

4.  After a company announces trouble, act early, because exits close fast.

## 6. Behavioural traps

The hardest part of investing is managing your own behaviour. These habits are well documented and affect experts as well as beginners.

| Trap | What it looks like | Antidote |
|----|----|----|
| Fear of missing out (FOMO) | Buying a stock because it just rose and everyone is talking about it | Decide your buy price before looking |
| Loss aversion | Holding a loser to avoid admitting the loss, selling winners too soon | Ask: would I buy this today at this price? |
| Overconfidence | A few early wins feel like skill | Keep a record of every trade and its result |
| Anchoring | Fixating on your buy price or a past high | The market does not know what you paid |
| Herd behaviour | Following the crowd, tips and social media | Check the facts yourself |
| Confirmation bias | Reading only views that agree with you | Look for the best argument against |
| Recency bias | Assuming what happened lately will continue | Read long-term history |
| Sunk cost | Adding money to rescue a past mistake | Judge future prospects only |
| Panic selling | Selling at the bottom in a crash | A written plan and a rule for crashes |
| Gambler's fallacy | Believing a stock is due to turn | A price has no memory |

**Worked example: averaging a loser.** You buy at 100. It falls to 60. You buy more to lower your average, then more at 30. Your average is 63. If the company is failing, you have put three times as much into the loss. The question is not whether the price is lower, but whether you would start a new position now.

**Worked example: the cost of panic.** Between 8 January and 27 October 2008 the Nifty fell from 6,288 to 2,524, a fall of 59.9%. A year later, on 27 October 2009, it closed at 4,847, up 92% from the low. An investor who sold at the bottom locked in the loss and missed the recovery. The point is not to call the bottom, which no one can do reliably, but to size positions so you are never forced to sell.

**Tips from other people.** A tip is a story without evidence. Ask what the person gains, whether they hold the stock, and whether they will tell you when they sell.

**Decision journal.** Before each buy, write one paragraph: what you bought, why, what would make you wrong, and the price at which you will reconsider. Reread it when you feel tempted to act.

**Check your state.** Avoid trading when angry, tired, or after a big win or loss. Many mistakes happen right after a gain or a loss.

## 7. Scams, manipulation and false promises

Fraud in investing usually looks like an opportunity. The common thread is a promise that is too good, from a person you do not know, with pressure to act quickly.

| Scam | How it works | Warning signs |
|----|----|----|
| Tips groups (WhatsApp, Telegram, YouTube) | A stranger gives 'sure' stock tips; some groups profit by selling to followers after the price rises | Guaranteed returns, urgency, paid memberships, fake screenshots |
| Pump and dump | A small stock is hyped and sold to the crowd | Sudden rise on no news and thin volume |
| Fake trading apps | An unregistered app shows invented profits; withdrawals fail | Link from a stranger; app not from a registered broker |
| IPO and pre-IPO offers | Unlisted shares sold at a promised listing price | Offers outside the exchange system |
| Unregistered advisers | Someone charges for advice without SEBI registration | No registration number; claims of fixed returns |
| Portfolio management fraud | A person manages your money and sends false statements | Cannot show Demat statements in your own name |
| Impersonation | Scammers use the name of known firms or officials | Calls or messages asking for OTPs or transfers |
| Ponzi schemes | Returns paid from new deposits | Fixed high monthly returns |

**Rules that protect you.**

1.  Trade only through a SEBI-registered broker. Check the registration on the SEBI or exchange website.

2.  Take advice only from SEBI-registered investment advisers or research analysts. Verify their registration number.

3.  Never send money to a personal account for a trade or an investment.

4.  Never share OTPs, passwords or screen-sharing access.

5.  Your shares should always show in a Demat account in your name.

6.  Assume that a guaranteed return is false.

**Worked example: why a guaranteed return fails.** A scheme promises 5% a month. Compounded, that is about 80% a year. If it could deliver that reliably, it would not need your small deposit. Real long-run equity returns are far lower, with large falls in between.

**How a tip group profits.** A group builds a large following. The leader buys a thin stock, tells followers to buy, and sells into the rise. The followers are the buyers on the other side. Ask who gains if you act.

**If you have been cheated.**

1.  Stop sending money.

2.  Keep records: messages, bank transfers, screenshots.

3.  Report quickly to the cyber crime helpline (1930 in India) and your bank.

4.  File a complaint with SEBI through its SCORES portal if a registered entity is involved, and with the police.

5.  Do not pay anyone who promises to recover your money for a fee.

**Final test.** If you cannot explain, in one sentence, how the investment makes money, do not buy it.

## 8. Crashes in context: 2008 and 2020

Markets fall hard from time to time, and every investor should know roughly how hard and how long. The Nifty record in this project's NSE data begins in September 2007 and shows two large falls.

|                       | 2008                    | 2020                     |
|-----------------------|-------------------------|--------------------------|
| Peak                  | 6,287.85 on 8 Jan 2008  | 12,224.55 on 20 Jan 2020 |
| Lowest close          | 2,524.20 on 27 Oct 2008 | 7,610.25 on 23 Mar 2020  |
| Fall                  | 59.9%                   | 37.7%                    |
| Days from peak to low | 293                     | 63                       |
| Regained the old peak | 9 Nov 2010 (6,301.55)   | 6 Nov 2020 (12,263.55)   |
| Worst single day      | −12.2% on 24 Oct 2008   | −13.0% on 23 Mar 2020    |

The 2008 fall took longer to heal: nearly three years from the peak to a new high, against under ten months in 2020. The recovery pace differs each time, so no timeline can be assumed.

**Calendar-year changes in the Nifty 50 index (price only, dividends excluded, measured from each year's first close to its last close).** The data shows the range that has actually occurred.

| Year | Change | Year | Change |
|------|--------|------|--------|
| 2008 | −52%   | 2015 | −5%    |
| 2009 | +71%   | 2016 | +5%    |
| 2010 | +17%   | 2017 | +29%   |
| 2011 | −25%   | 2018 | +4%    |
| 2012 | +24%   | 2019 | +13%   |
| 2013 | +5%    | 2020 | +15%   |
| 2014 | +33%   | 2021 | +24%   |

For 2007 and 2026 the data covers only part of the year, so they are left out. Taking the whole period, the index rose from 4,495 on 17 September 2007 to 22,623 on 14 October 2026, about 8.8% a year before dividends. That long-run figure hides a year of −52% and a year of +71%.

**What most stocks did.** Index figures flatter the typical stock. Among the 889 clean collapses in our data, peak years cluster: 186 peaked in 2008, 99 in 2007, 96 in 2010, 91 in 2017 and 2018 together, 102 in 2021 and 2022 together, and 91 in 2024. Many peaked at the top of a boom and never came back.

**Lessons.**

1.  A fall of 38% to 60% in the index has happened twice in 19 years. Plan for it.

2.  Falls and recoveries are fast. The gain in the year after the 2008 low was 92%.

3.  You need money you will not have to sell. Keep your emergency fund outside shares.

4.  An index recovers, a single stock may not.

5.  Do not extrapolate. A year of +71% and a year of −52% are both in the data.

## 9. Habits that help

Good outcomes mostly come from a few boring habits followed for years. None needs special knowledge.

**1. Diversify across assets and across stocks.** Hold a mix such as shares, bonds, gold and cash, and within shares hold many companies or an index fund. Diversification does not remove risk. It stops one failure from ending the plan.

**2. Decide your asset allocation, then rebalance.** Pick a split, such as 60% shares and 40% bonds, that matches your goal and your ability to bear loss. Once a year, sell what has grown too large and buy what has shrunk, to restore the split.

| Example | Start  | After shares rise 30%, bonds flat | Rebalanced |
|---------|--------|-----------------------------------|------------|
| Shares  | 60,000 | 78,000                            | 70,800     |
| Bonds   | 40,000 | 40,000                            | 47,200     |

In the example, the portfolio grew to 1,18,000. At 60/40 the shares should be 70,800 and bonds 47,200, so you move 7,200 from shares to bonds.

**3. Size every position.** Limit one stock to about 5% of your savings and one sector to about 20%. Smaller positions are easier to hold through a fall.

**4. Invest regularly.** A monthly SIP removes the guessing game and builds the habit.

**5. Keep costs low.** Direct plans, index funds, few trades. A fee of 1% a year compounds against you.

**6. Keep a decision journal.** Write why you bought, what would prove you wrong, and when you will review.

**7. Review on a schedule.** Look at your portfolio quarterly, not daily. Daily price watching raises stress and trading.

**8. Separate trading from investing.** If you must trade, use a small fixed amount you can afford to lose entirely.

**Before you buy: a checklist.**

I can explain in one sentence how this business makes money.

I have read the last annual report summary and the shareholding pattern.

Debt and cash flow look sound.

Average daily volume is high enough for me to exit.

The position is no more than 5% of my savings.

I have written what would make me sell.

**Before you sell: a checklist.**

Has the reason I bought changed?

Am I selling because of price, news or fear?

Do I have a better use for this money?

Have I counted tax and costs?

## 10. Tricks and tips: what holds up and what is myth

There is no trick that reliably beats the market. What exists are habits that reduce mistakes and costs. Use this list as a filter when you hear advice.

| Claim you will hear | Verdict | Why |
|----|----|----|
| 'Buy low, sell high' | True but useless | No one knows the low or the high in advance |
| 'Time in the market beats timing the market' | Broadly supported | Missing a few big days can cut long-run returns, and big days cluster near big falls |
| 'Diversify' | Holds up | Reduces the chance one failure ruins you |
| 'Keep costs low' | Holds up | Costs are certain; returns are not |
| 'Start early' | Holds up | Compounding needs time |
| 'Cut your losses, let profits run' | Mixed | Needs a rule you follow every time, and it fails in choppy markets |
| 'The trend is your friend' | Mixed | Trends exist until they end; hard to tell in advance |
| 'Cheap stocks always bounce back' | False | Many stocks never recover, as the 889 collapses show |
| 'Follow the smart money or insiders' | Weak | Filings come with delay; you see them after the move |
| 'Sell in May and go away' | Weak | A calendar rule without strong support in the long run |
| 'This time is different' | Be careful | Often the last words before a loss |
| 'Guaranteed returns' | False | A promise of certainty is a red flag |
| 'Astrological or numerological timing' | Untested unless shown | Needs the same strict testing as any other rule (section 12) |

**Worked example: missing the best days.** Two investors hold the Nifty. One stays invested. The other leaves for a few weeks in a panic and misses the 18 May 2009 rise of 17.7% and the 7 April 2020 rise of 8.8%. Each of those days alone added a large part of a year's return. The best days sat close to the worst, so an investor who sells in fear often misses them.

**Practical tips that do help.**

1.  Learn one thing at a time. Start with index funds, then add what you understand.

2.  Practise before risking money. Many brokers and the exchanges offer demo or paper trading.

3.  Read the contract note every time.

4.  Use limit orders for anything not very liquid.

5.  Never invest money you will need within three years.

6.  Be most careful when you feel certain.

7.  Write down your losses as carefully as your wins.

8.  If you do not understand a product, do not buy it.

**On information.** News is already in the price by the time you read it. Annual reports, filings and the shareholding pattern are more useful than headlines. Be sceptical of anything that tells you what the market will do next week.

**On being patient.** In this project's data, the Nifty needed nearly three years from its January 2008 peak to regain it. Patience is a plan only when your money does not have to be spent in the meantime.

## 11. Investor protection, complaints and official sources

India has a regulator, exchange systems and complaint channels for investors. Know where they are before you need them.

| Body | Role | What you use it for |
|----|----|----|
| SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India) | Regulates markets, brokers, advisers and funds | Check registrations; file complaints on SCORES |
| NSE and BSE | Run the exchanges | Company announcements, lot sizes, circulars, investor complaints about members |
| NSDL and CDSL | Depositories | Demat statements, nominee and account details |
| AMFI | Mutual fund industry body | Fund data, distributor registration |
| RBI | Banking regulator, bond market | Government bonds, bank rules |
| Income-tax Department | Tax | Filing returns, tax statements |
| Investor Education and Protection Fund | Holds unclaimed dividends and shares | Claiming old dividends |

**Checks before you trust anyone.**

1.  Broker: look up the SEBI registration and exchange membership.

2.  Adviser: SEBI-registered investment advisers and research analysts have a registration number you can verify.

3.  Mutual fund distributor: an AMFI registration number (ARN).

4.  Any app or website: confirm it is a registered broker's own.

**Complaint route.** Complain first to the company or broker and keep the reference. If you do not get an answer within the stated time, file on SEBI's SCORES portal, and for disputes use the arbitration or online dispute resolution routes the exchanges and SEBI provide. For fraud, also report to the police and the cyber crime helpline.

**What protection exists and what does not.**

- Shares in your Demat account are held in your name in the depository, not with your broker.

- Exchanges run investor protection funds that give limited compensation if a member defaults. The limits are modest.

- No scheme protects you from losses caused by market falls or by your own choices.

- Cash left with a broker is the part most at risk.

**Nominee and inheritance.**

1.  Nominate a person for every Demat account and mutual fund folio.

2.  Share the account details with a trusted family member.

3.  Keep a list of holdings, broker, depository and bank details in a safe place.

4.  If a holder dies, the nominee or legal heir claims the holdings through the depository with a death certificate and forms.

**Record keeping.** Keep contract notes, capital-gains reports and tax statements for at least 6 years.

**Official sources.** Check the SEBI, NSE and BSE websites for regulations and circulars, the AMFI website for fund data, and the Income-tax Department site for tax rules. Rules change, so confirm any rule with the original source.

## 12. How your own study relates to investing

The dasha and collapse study in this project is a research question, not a trading method, and nothing in these guides tells you to trade on astrology. It is useful here because it shows how to test any rule fairly.

**What the study does.** It asks whether the start of a lasting price collapse falls in particular dasha periods of a stock's first-trade chart more often than chance. The rules were fixed and registered before the test, on a frozen list of 206 stocks. The test itself has not been run yet.

**What a result would and would not mean.**

| If the test shows | It would mean | It would not mean |
|----|----|----|
| An association above chance | Collapse starts cluster in a period class across 206 stocks | That you can predict a collapse, or profit from it |
| No association | Any effect is smaller than about 9 percentage points | That astrology is proven wrong in every form |

**Earlier results.** An earlier test of one rule, the Tara Bala from the first-trade star, returned 'not supported' under the same rules. A first exploratory check of 72 companies found no separation (Fisher p = 0.48).

**Why this matters to an investor.** The study is built around the same traps as this guide.

1.  **Survivorship**: it includes stocks that later disappeared.

2.  **Hindsight**: the collapse is known only afterwards, so each stock is compared with its own other dates.

3.  **Multiple testing**: only two tests were declared, and the count was fixed in advance.

4.  **Power**: a test that cannot find a small effect says little about whether one exists.

5.  **Costs and tradability**: even a real association would need to be big enough to profit after costs, and available before the event, not after.

**A use for the lessons.** Next time you hear a rule such as 'buy when X happens', ask these questions: how many rules were tried, on what data, was the rule fixed before the test, and does it survive costs?

**Keep the two apart.** Your research can be as curious as you like. Your savings should follow the plan in sections 9 and 10, which does not depend on any single rule being true.

**Self-check.**

**Answers.**

1.  20%.

2.  Lenders can sell the pledged shares in a fall, which pushes the price down further.

3.  1,022 of 3,616, about 28%.

4.  You may be unable to sell for days.

5.  Until 9 November 2010, nearly three years.

6.  No investment can guarantee returns; the promise is how frauds attract money.

7.  Sell some shares and buy bonds to restore 60/40.

8.  How many rules were tried, on what data, was the rule fixed before the test, and does it survive costs?
