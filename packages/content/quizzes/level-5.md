## Q1 | lessons: currency-and-commodities

You buy one USD/INR lot (1,000 dollars) at 80.00 and the rate moves to 81.50. What is the gain before charges, and what would a move to 78.50 have cost?

### Answer

1.50 x 1,000 = ₹1,500 gained. A move to 78.50 would have cost the same, ₹1,500.

## Q2 | lessons: currency-and-commodities

If you buy a USD/INR future, do you gain when the rupee gets stronger or weaker?

### Answer

Weaker. You gain when the dollar costs more rupees, which is the rate rising.

## Q3 | lessons: currency-and-commodities

A currency margin is small and the daily moves are small. Why is it still easy to take too much risk?

### Answer

The small margin lets you hold many lots, and a run of one-way days adds up. Ten lots lose ₹20,000 on a two-rupee move. Size by that loss, not by the margin.

## Q4 | lessons: rates-and-the-bond-seesaw

A 10-year 7% bond sells at 100 when the yield is 7%. What happens to its price if the yield rises to 8%, and why?

### Answer

It falls, to about 93.29. New bonds pay more, so the old 7% coupon is worth less.

## Q5 | lessons: rates-and-the-bond-seesaw

A bond fund has a modified duration of 6. Roughly what happens to its price if yields rise one point?

### Answer

It falls by about 6%.

## Q6 | lessons: global-forces

Name two ways a weaker rupee can change the earnings of Indian companies.

### Answer

Importers such as oil buyers pay more rupees for the same goods, so their costs rise. Exporters earn more rupees for the same dollars, so their earnings can rise.
