A derivative position can lose more than the money you put in, and the market's own history shows how fast that can happen.

**Who pays margin.** Buyers of options pay only the premium. Sellers of options and buyers or sellers of futures must deposit margin, which the exchange sets from the risk of a bad day. The broker may demand more if the market moves against you. If the account falls below the margin, you must add money or the position is closed at the market price, often at the worst moment.

**Real daily moves in the Nifty (NSE data, from the guide).** +17.7% on 18 May 2009, -13.0% on 23 March 2020, -12.2% on 24 October 2008, +8.8% on 7 April 2020. Moves of 5% or more in a day happened on 43 of about 4,680 trading days, roughly one in 109, and they cluster in crises.

**Worked example: a short straddle in a jump.** A seller sells the 22,600 straddle for 290 a unit (one lot of 65) with an assumed margin of about 2.5 lakh rupees. If the index jumps 4,000 points to 26,600, the call loses 4,000 a unit at expiry. The net loss is 4,000 - 290 = 3,710 a unit, or 2,41,150 rupees on 65 units, about equal to the margin. In a larger event the loss would exceed the deposit.

**Why leverage cuts both ways.** A lot controlling 14.7 lakh rupees needs about 1.8 lakh of margin, so the leverage is about 8 times. A 5% move changes the position by about 73,000 rupees, about 42% of the margin.

**Rules of thumb.** Never put in margin you cannot afford to lose. Assume the worst move in the table could happen. Prefer defined-risk positions. The app's mistake checker flags margin used above 50% of the account.

**Costs.** Derivative trades are cheap each but frequent. Brokerage is a flat fee per order, often about 20 rupees. STT on futures is on the sell side (0.05% of the traded value as reported after April 2026) and on options it is 0.15% of the premium on the sell side. Add the exchange charge, GST of 18% on brokerage and exchange charge, and stamp duty on the buy side.

**Worked example: costs on one options trade.** Buy one lot of 65 at a premium of 120 (7,800 rupees) and sell at 150 (9,750 rupees). Gross profit 1,950. Brokerage for two orders 40, STT on the sale 15, exchange charges 6, GST and stamp duty 8: total about 69. Net profit about 1,881. Costs took about 3.5% of the gain. On a trade that gains little or loses, the same charges apply.

**Tax on F&O income.** F&O profit or loss is non-speculative business income, added to your other income and taxed at your slab rate, not at capital-gains rates. You file the return form that allows business income, not the simple one. A business loss can be set off against other business income and carried forward for 8 years if you file on time, but generally not against salary. Turnover follows special rules and a tax audit may be needed above certain limits. STT is not an allowed cost when you work out capital gains on shares, but for F&O business income it is generally allowed as an expense. The investor guide says it cannot be claimed, so a reviewer should confirm this point with a tax adviser.

**Worked example: tax and loss.** Lose 3 lakh rupees in F&O in a year and earn a 12 lakh salary: the loss does not reduce your salary tax. It can reduce future F&O profits for up to 8 years, but only if reported. Keep contract notes and the broker's yearly statement. These rates change often, so check a current tax source.
