Short selling bets on a price falling, and it carries a risk that buying does not: the loss has no upper limit. The way you enter the market also changes your result more than most people expect.

**Short selling.**

- **Intraday short**: sell first, buy back the same day. Allowed for retail investors.
- **Delivery-based short**: not allowed for retail. Selling shares you do not hold is restricted.
- **Derivatives**: futures and options are the usual way to bet on a fall.

Why the risk is unlimited: if you sell at 100 and the price rises to 300, you lose 200 per share. A share can only fall to zero, so the most a buyer can lose is the amount paid. A short seller can lose many times the starting amount. The app does not offer short selling in practice trading for this reason.

**Ways to enter.**

| Method | How it works | Strength | Weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lump sum | Invest all at once | Money is working from day one | If you pick a peak, you wait a long time |
| SIP / staggered | Invest equal amounts regularly | Reduces timing risk; builds habit | Lower gains if the market only rises |
| Averaging down | Buy more as price falls | Lowers average cost | Dangerous if the business has really failed |
| Averaging up | Add as price rises | Adds to winners | Higher cost per share |

**Worked example: averaging down on a failing company.** You buy 100 shares of a company at 100, then 100 more at 70 and 100 more at 40. Your cost is 10,000 + 7,000 + 4,000 = 21,000 rupees, an average of 70 a share. If the price then drops to 3 rupees, your 300 shares are worth 900. Cheaper does not mean better value. Averaging makes sense only when your reason for owning the business is still valid.

**Worked example: SIP across a crash.** A monthly SIP of 10,000 rupees into a fund at unit prices 100, 80, 60, 80 and 100 buys 100, 125, 167, 125 and 100 units, a total of 617 units for 50,000 rupees. The average cost is 81, below the average price of 84. The gain comes from buying more units when the price was low.

**Position size.** Many experienced investors limit any single share to a small share of their portfolio, often 5% or less. If it fails, the portfolio survives.

**Avoid chasing.** Buying after a stock has risen 50% in a few weeks is a common source of regret. Decide your price range before the market opens.
