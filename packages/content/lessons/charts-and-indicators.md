Technical analysis studies price and volume. It gives clear entry and exit rules, but its patterns are easy to see in hindsight and often fail later. Learn the tools so you can read a chart, and keep their limits in mind.

**A candle** shows one day (or one week) of trading: the open, the high, the low and the close. The thick part, the body, runs from the open to the close. The thin lines, the wicks, reach the day's high and low. In the app a green candle closed above its open and a red one closed below it.

**Moving average.** The average of the last N closing prices, recalculated each day. A 20-day average follows the price closely. A 50-day average is slower. A price above its 200-day average is called an uptrend.

**Worked example: a moving average.** Five closes of 100, 102, 104, 106 and 108 add up to 520. The 5-day average is 520 / 5 = 104. When the next close is 110, the oldest value (100) drops out and the new average is (102 + 104 + 106 + 108 + 110) / 5 = 106. An average always trails the price, because it is made from past prices.

**RSI (relative strength index).** A gauge from 0 to 100 that compares recent gains with recent losses over, usually, 14 days. Readings above 70 are called overbought and below 30 oversold. These are names, not orders: a strong stock can stay above 70 for weeks.

**Worked example: RSI.** If the average daily gain over the period is 1.0 and the average daily loss is 0.5, the ratio is 2. RSI = 100 - 100 / (1 + 2) = 66.7. If gains and losses were equal, the ratio would be 1 and the RSI 50.

**Volume** is the number of shares traded. A move on heavy volume has many participants behind it. A move on thin volume can be reversed by a few traders. Volume also tells you whether you can sell when you want to (see the lesson on the order book).

**Support and resistance** are price levels where buyers (support) or sellers (resistance) have appeared before. They are easy to draw after the fact and unreliable in advance.

**Adjusted prices.** When a company splits its shares, the price drops on the day although nobody has lost money. Charts for study use prices adjusted for splits and bonuses, so a split does not look like a crash. The app does this on its charts.

**What the evidence says.** Careful research tests a rule on data it was not designed on and counts how many rules were tried. Many published patterns lose their edge once costs are included, and a pattern that worked in one period may fail in the next. You can see a pattern in almost any chart.

**A useful way to use a chart.** Use price and volume to avoid illiquid stocks and to avoid buying into a collapse. Write down your rule for getting in and out before you trade.
