A bond pays a fixed coupon. Its price changes every day, so the return you earn depends on what you pay. The yield to maturity is the single yearly rate that makes the bond's cash flows worth the price you pay today.

**The see-saw.** When market yields rise, existing bonds with lower coupons become less attractive, so their prices fall. When yields fall, prices rise. A bond bought at face value and held to maturity still pays back the face value, but the price in between moves.

**Worked example.** A 10-year bond pays a 7% coupon twice a year. If the market yield is 7%, it sells at its face value of 100. If the yield rises to 8%, the price becomes about 93.29. If it falls to 6%, the price rises above 100. Try this on the Bonds screen in Practise.

**Duration.** Modified duration tells you roughly how many percent the price changes for each one point change in yield. A duration of 7 means a one point rise in yield cuts the price by about 7%. Longer bonds and lower coupons have a higher duration, so they are hit harder when rates rise.

**Current yield and yield to maturity.** Current yield is the coupon divided by the price. It ignores the gain or loss at maturity. Yield to maturity includes it, so it is the better single number.

**Debt funds.** A debt fund holds many bonds and has its own duration. A long-duration fund falls when rates rise even though it holds government bonds. Check the fund's average maturity or duration before you buy.

**Habit.** Before buying a bond or debt fund, ask what happens to its price if yields rise one point.
