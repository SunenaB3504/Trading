Risk means the chance of losing money or not reaching your goal. It comes in several kinds, and the biggest one for a stock buyer is the one beginners think least about: the company itself failing.

| Risk | Meaning | Example |
|----|----|----|
| Market risk | The whole market falls | Nifty fell 59.9% from January to October 2008 |
| Company risk | One business does badly | Yes Bank fell 97.3% from August 2018 to August 2021 |
| Concentration risk | Too much money in one stock or sector | Holding only one of the names that collapsed |
| Liquidity risk | You cannot sell without moving the price | Aban Offshore traded only 9,969 shares on 16 October 2026 |
| Leverage risk | Borrowed money magnifies a loss | MTF and F&O (Guides 2 and 3) |
| Inflation risk | Returns do not keep up with prices | Savings account at 4% against 6% inflation |
| Interest-rate risk | Bond prices fall when rates rise | Guide 1, section 6 |
| Currency risk | The rupee moves against a foreign asset | Foreign funds and shares |
| Regulatory risk | A rule change hurts a business or product | Tax changes, SEBI orders |
| Operational risk | Mistakes, outages, fraud | Wrong order, broker failure |

**How common is a collapse?** In this project we examined every NSE stock with at least 250 trading days between 2007 and October 2026, which is 3,616 stocks. Of them:

- **1,022 stocks (28%)** fell at least 70% from a peak, never regained half of that peak, and ended the data at 40% or less of it.

- **889 of those** had no unexplained one-day drop worse than 35%, which makes a corporate event such as a merger less likely to be the cause.

These are not 889 frauds. Many were small, thin companies, and some were ordinary failures of a business. They show that a long-lived stock falling 70% and staying down is a common outcome in this market, not a freak event.

**Survivorship and this evidence.** The Nifty 50 index continually replaces failing companies with stronger ones, so its history looks better than the history of a fixed set of stocks. An investor who held the same 50 names from 2007 would have had a different result.

**Diversification.** Holding many unrelated stocks reduces company risk. If one in four stocks can fail badly, a portfolio of three stocks has a good chance of including one failure. A broad index fund holds hundreds of companies and updates the list.

**Worked example: a small portfolio.** You put 1,00,000 rupees equally in 5 stocks, 20,000 each. One falls 90%, and the other four rise 10% each. Result: 18,000 + 4 times 22,000 = 1,06,000. A single stock that falls 90% would have cost you 90,000.

**Position sizing.** If you cap each stock at 5% of your savings, a 90% loss costs 4.5% of the total. That is survivable. At 50% it costs 45%.
